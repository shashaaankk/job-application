# Scope

This file holds what he believes about engineering. [05. profile](05.%20profile.md) holds what he has done, and [knowledge](knowledge/) holds the evidence behind it. A cover letter's motivation comes from here and from nowhere else.

Three rules govern its use.

1. An entry is interpreted into a letter, never quoted. The wording here is source material for grounding and for interviews, and a letter carries the position rephrased for that company's work in fresh words each time.
2. Each entry's applicability line is binding. Outside its scope the entry does not exist for that letter. Stretching one into a domain it does not name puts a belief in his mouth that he never held about that domain.
3. A conviction is never derived from what he did. If no in-scope entry exists, the motivation rests on plain continuity, or he is asked.

# 1. Think in layers, and spend hardware where it removes difficulty

Humans are limited by evolution and robots are not. An eye at the back of the head builds a full 360 degree egocentric view. You need to think in layers. If engineers are struggling with SLAM while the grip problem sits unsolved, the effort is misallocated. Hardware should remove difficulty from the perception layer so that scarce engineering concentrates on what is genuinely hard for robots, which is touch and manipulation.

What makes this defensible rather than an opinion. His thesis measured memory bandwidth as the bound on viable on-device models, so he can argue which added pixels justify their compute cost instead of proposing sensors naively.

Applies to humanoid and mobile robotics roles.

Deployment rule. Stated as where he would spend a sensing budget, never as telling the employer their anatomy is wrong.

# 2. Autonomous driving, the modular school, with an honest bridge to end-to-end

He is for the modular school, with explicit safety monitors, mainly for explainability. Software development is an evolutionary process, and hoping to get things right the first time is naive, resource intensive and bad for time to market.

This does not stop him applying to end-to-end companies. At one, his angle is bringing assurance and explainability thinking into a learned stack.

Interview note. The evolutionary-development argument is also claimed by the end-to-end school, which iterates by retraining on data. Expect that pushback.

Applies to ADAS and autonomous driving roles on either side of the divide.

# 3. Patient consequence, and checks over perfect functions

Answered in layers. Anything that harms the user of the solution must be avoided. Any probability that the solution harms other parties must be thoroughly investigated, especially when life is involved. Building a system that works is not enough. The functions might be perfect, but the uncertainties that come with them must be considered and compensated. Put checks in place.

Applies to medical, safety critical and human adjacent roles.

# 4. Unattended machines, where the hard problem is uncertainty

Uncertainties. Machines are built to solve a problem and are ever improving, but they need uncertainty management. A supervisory layer, like the one he built in his thesis, solves this problem.

Applies to field robotics, agricultural robotics, and any autonomy that runs without an operator.

# The through-line

All four entries are one belief. Perfect functions are not enough, and uncertainty has to be characterised and supervised by explicit layers.

His record is that belief practiced before he named it. Allan deviation noise characterisation on the IMU, injected network impairments to find where a control loop breaks, MC/DC coverage on shipped automotive code, and a thesis that is a supervisory uncertainty layer for an autonomous robot.
